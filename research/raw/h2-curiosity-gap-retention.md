## FINDINGS

### 1. The mechanism, stated correctly (Loewenstein 1994, the source everyone paraphrases)

George Loewenstein formulated information-gap theory in 1994 ("The Psychology of Curiosity: A
Review and Reinterpretation," Psychological Bulletin). Core claim, verbatim from the secondary
sources that quote it: curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's
knowledge," a gap "between what we know and what we want to know." The gap creates an aversive
state (like deprivation, hunger) that the brain is compelled to close. Curiosity is triggered
the MOMENT you become aware of the gap, so the job of a cover slide is not to inform, it is to
make the viewer AWARE they are missing something.

Two corollaries that matter for design, both from Loewenstein via the CMU / Golman line and the
Psychology Fanatic summary:
- Curiosity intensity scales with how CLOSE you feel to the answer. A gap you feel one swipe
  from closing is more compelling than a gap that feels far off. This is why "wait until slide
  7" works: it puts a finite, visible boundary on the gap.
- "Surprising, novel, incongruous, complex, and ambiguous stimuli induce curiosity ONLY when
  one recognizes an information gap." Novelty alone is not enough. The stimulus has to make you
  feel you are missing a specific piece of knowledge. TIER 1.

Loewenstein's four central issues of curiosity (definition/dimensionality, cause, voluntary
exposure, situational determinants; plus a fifth on superficiality/intensity) are frequently
mis-cited online as "four triggers." They are not a trigger list. Do not repeat the "four
triggers" framing as if it were Loewenstein's. TIER 1 correction.

### 2. The single most important primary finding: curiosity gaps are CURVILINEAR, not "always win"

This is the best, most citable, most surprising thing I found, and it directly contradicts the
lazy "always be vague to force the swipe" advice.

Aubin Le Quere and Matias, "When curiosity gaps backfire: effects of headline concreteness on
information selection decisions," Scientific Reports (Nature), published 2025-01-06. Meta-analysis
of 8,977 headline experiments run on Upworthy (2013-2015 archive). TIER 1.

Findings, exact numbers:
- The relationship between headline concreteness and clickthrough is U-shaped / curvilinear, NOT
  monotonic. There is a sweet spot at MODERATE concreteness (their scale, 2.58 to 3.06).
- When a headline is too VAGUE, adding concreteness INCREASES clicks (about +5.5% at the vague
  end).
- When a headline is too CONCRETE, adding more concreteness DECREASES clicks (about -9.9% at the
  concrete end).
- Distribution of the tested headlines: only 8.7% were too vague (would benefit from more
  specificity); 50.9% were OVER-concrete (would perform better if made vaguer).
- Conclusion the authors give: "headlines that convey just the right amount of information
  maximize clickthrough rates at scale." Curiosity-gap headlines sometimes beat summary
  headlines and sometimes lose; concreteness level is the moderator that resolves the old
  conflicting-results debate.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR US: the majority failure mode in the real world is being TOO concrete
(over half of headlines), i.e. the cover slide answers its own question and kills the swipe.
But the opposite extreme (pure mystery, no anchor) also loses. The target is a partial reveal:
enough concrete detail to signal the payoff is real and specific, with exactly one thing
withheld. "5.5% up vs 9.9% down" is asymmetric: over-concreteness costs almost double what
over-vagueness costs, so if you must err, err slightly vague. TIER 1, highest-value finding in
this division.

### 3. Zeigarnik / open loops: real as a driver, but the 2025 meta-analysis demands honesty

The open-loop story everyone tells: Bluma Zeigarnik (1927, Berlin) found waiters remembered
unpaid orders far better than settled ones; interrupted tasks recalled ~90% better than
completed ones; unfinished tasks sit in working memory as "open loops" the brain prioritizes
for closure. Infinite scroll and cliffhanger serialization exploit this. That is the standard
neuromarketing pitch (blog.neuromarket.co, 2024-10-29). TIER 2 for the marketing framing, but
the underlying Zeigarnik study is TIER 1.

CRITICAL HONESTY FLAG: a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of the accumulated Zeigarnik
and Ovsiankina literature found NO reliable MEMORY advantage for unfinished tasks, but DID find
a general tendency to RESUME interrupted tasks. Read that carefully. It does not say open loops
do nothing. It says the mechanism is probably not "you remember it better," it is "you feel a
pull to GO BACK AND FINISH IT." For a carousel that is arguably even better framing: we do not
need viewers to remember slide 3, we need them to feel compelled to swipe to slide 4 to close
the loop. The resumption drive is the swipe drive. But we must stop claiming the "90% better
recall" number as settled science, because the 2025 meta-analysis undercuts the memory claim
specifically. Use "resumption pull," not "you'll remember it." TIER 1 caveat.

### 4. Slide 2 is the highest-value action, and slide 1 to slide 2 is the steepest cliff

Consistent across every Tier 2 source: the swipe from slide 1 to slide 2 is the single most
critical friction point. If a viewer does not swipe near-immediately, the algorithm reads it as
disinterest and buries the post. A drop on slide 2 is diagnosed as a HOOK problem, not a content
problem (marketingagent.blog; trymypost). Restated: slide 1 earns the pause, slide 2 earns the
commitment to the rest of the deck. TIER 2.

Reported retention-curve SHAPE (consistent across creators per the aggregating blogs, even
though absolute percentages vary by niche): the steepest drop happens between slides 1 and 3;
viewers who push PAST slide 4 tend to stay because "the retention curve flattens dramatically"
(imgshifter, creatorflow, aggregated in search). This gives a clean design rule: the first 3-4
slides must front-load open loops and payoff, because that is where you are bleeding people. By
slide 4 the survivors are committed. TIER 2, but the curve shape is reported consistently enough
to design around.

### 5. The per-slide "swipe-through" numbers, all Tier 2, ranges not points

Collecting every specific figure I actually saw, with its source, so we can design against a
range rather than one blog's guess:
- "Target 65%+ swipe-through rate" (search aggregation on trymypost / marketingagent). TIER 2.
- "A strong first slide gets 60% or more of viewers to swipe"; "slide 1 carries 60% of the
  swipe-through rate" (TikTok photo-mode aggregation, postwaffle / reelbase). TIER 2.
- "First slide carries ~80% of the weight" of overall swipe-through (postnitro, 2025-12-15;
  also truefuturemedia). TIER 2. Note this conflicts with the "60%" figure above; treat "the
  cover slide dominates, somewhere 60-80% of the outcome" as the honest read.
- "Good swipe-through rate range: 15-25%"; "completion rate target: 40-50% reaching final
  slide" (postnitro, 2025-12-15, explicitly labeled a general benchmark, not measured). TIER 2.
- LinkedIn document carousels: average completion rate ~36% for well-structured carousels
  (postunreel aggregation). TIER 2.
- "If 60% of viewers reach the final slide, Instagram interprets it as high-value and pushes to
  Explore" (marketingagent.blog). TIER 2.

DESIGN TAKEAWAY from the spread: nobody agrees on the exact number because nobody can see each
other's private Insights (see FINDING 8). But the DIRECTION is unanimous: cover slide decides
most of the outcome, ~half your reach is lost by the final slide even on good decks, and
completion rate is the metric the algorithm rewards.

### 6. The distribution multiplier: the reason retention pacing is worth obsessing over

Repeated claim, attributed to a "leaked 2024 Meta internal report": carousels where 70%+ of
viewers see 70%+ of slides are distributed to 3-5x more non-followers than low-swipe carousels
(postnitro / carousel-post aggregation). TIER 2, and I could NOT verify the leaked-report claim
against a primary document (see GAPS). But even discounted, the qualitative point is echoed
everywhere: high completion rate is the lever that unlocks non-follower reach. That is exactly
the outcome Carter cares about (the 47k-65k view / thousands-of-saves decks). Retention pacing
is not vanity, it is the reach multiplier. TIER 2.

Corollary "unseen slides" mechanic (truefuturemedia, marketingagent): when a follower does NOT
swipe to the end, Instagram treats the unseen slides as "new content" and can re-serve the same
carousel starting from a different slide. This rewards LONGER decks with strong later slides,
because it creates multiple re-serve entry points. Cuts slightly against the "keep it short"
advice. TIER 2.

### 7. Numbered-list psychology: odd numbers, and WHY they help retention specifically

For a carousel that is literally "N things," the count is a design variable. Findings:
- Odd-numbered titles measured at +21% clicks vs an identical even-numbered version, with longer
  on-page time (blogherald / talkedaboutmarketing aggregation). TIER 2 for the exact 21%.
- Mechanism 1, chunking: information grouped in 3s or 5s is absorbed and RETAINED better (ties to
  working-memory chunking). This is a genuine retention lever, not just a click lever.
- Mechanism 2, authenticity signal: odd numbers read as "created from the material available"
  rather than padded to a round number, so they feel more human and less manufactured. Round
  numbers "are boring; odd numbers stand out."
- Mechanism 3, boundary relief: a numbered count sets a FINITE, visible boundary ("7 things"),
  which is itself a curiosity-gap object. You know there are exactly 7, you have seen 3, the gap
  is quantified and feels closable. This is the same finite-boundary mechanic as "wait until
  slide 7" in FINDING 1. TIER 2 with a Tier 1 chunking basis.

DESIGN TAKEAWAY: prefer odd counts (5, 7, 9). The count is not decoration, it is a
progress-bar-shaped curiosity gap. Showing "3 / 7" is a legitimate retention device.

### 8. HARD LIMIT: you cannot see anyone else's per-slide retention (why real A/B data is scarce)

sociavault.com states it plainly: saves, reach, and "swipe-through rate" all live in the post
owner's PRIVATE Insights and cannot be scraped. Public data exposes only like_count,
comment_count, and carousel_media_count. So third-party tools cannot produce competitor
per-slide drop-off curves. This is the structural reason nearly all "60% swipe to slide 2"
numbers are benchmarks passed hand-to-hand between blogs rather than audited measurements, and
why almost no creator-published A/B test with a real before/after per-slide curve exists in the
open. TIER 2 (but it is a statement about the API, so it is effectively verifiable). This is our
biggest evidence gap and it is a PLATFORM limitation, not a search failure.

The one bright spot: Instagram is rolling out native per-slide insights in 2026 (which slide got
each like, swipe-through and completion by slide), per multiple 2026 blogs. It is not universal
yet. When Carter's account has it, WE can run the real A/B tests nobody else can publish, on our
own decks. That is a concrete future capability, not something to buy today. TIER 2.

### 9. Saves are the deliberation signal; follows come from the finite-gap-closed feeling

Why saves (Carter's north-star metric): a like is a reflex double-tap; a SAVE requires
deliberation, so it is a far stronger quality signal, and the save is quiet (no notification),
so people use it freely to bank things they intend to return to (later.com, weswwim, hootsuite
experiment aggregation). Carousels reportedly get ~9x more saves than single images. Posts with
high saves and low likes often out-reach high-like low-save posts over time. TIER 2.

Why follows: the Zeigarnik/curiosity loop plus micro-commitment / sunk-cost. Sagum
(2026-03-18): each swipe is a psychological investment; users completing 4+ swipes showed 3.7x
higher conversion vs 2-card viewers, and high-converting carousels showed 41% higher engagement
on later cards. The follow is the viewer saying "this source reliably closes gaps I care about,
give me more." That means the LAST slide matters for follows: it should prove the gap was
closed well AND open a small new one ("next drop covers X") to justify the follow. TIER 2 for
Sagum's specific multipliers (single-source, treat as directional).

### 10. Concrete open-loop / cliffhanger mechanics that map to slide structure

Synthesized from the open-loop and cliffhanger sources (neuromarket.co; thuthuattienich carousel
cliffhanger; postnitro storytelling):
- MICRO-LOOPS vs MACRO-LOOPS. A macro-loop opens on the cover and closes only on the payoff
  slide ("the answer is on slide 7"). Micro-loops open and close within adjacent slides (each
  slide ends by teasing the next). Best decks LAYER both: one macro-loop spanning the whole deck
  plus a chain of micro-loops carrying each swipe. TIER 2.
- Named destination beats vague tease. "Wait until slide 7. You won't believe [outcome]" gives a
  finite target (ties to FINDING 1: intensity scales with felt closeness). Vagueness is
  intentional but must be ANCHORED, not total (ties to FINDING 2: over-vague loses too). TIER 2.
- Payoff proportionality. "A flat reveal violates the trust invested"; the payoff must be
  proportionate to the tension built. Over-teasing then under-delivering trains the audience to
  stop swiping your future decks. This is a retention-across-POSTS concern, not just within one.
  TIER 2.
- Do not open a loop you never close. Balance loops so the deck resolves; unresolved loops read
  as manipulative and cost trust. TIER 2.

### 11. Cognitive-timing and load numbers worth one experiment each (Sagum, single-source)

Sagum (2026-03-18) gives specific but single-source figures. Treat as hypotheses to test on our
own decks, NOT as established fact:
- Pattern disruption (deliberately breaking the deck's own visual rhythm partway): +34%
  swipe-through, +23% CTR vs a perfectly uniform/linear deck.
- Minimal cards with 50%+ negative space per slide: +28% CTR in competitive niches. (This
  directly supports Carter's poster-not-newspaper thesis.)
- "Native" un-designed look (screenshot-like): 2.3x engagement vs a slick ad-looking deck. (This
  one likely does NOT transfer to Carter's premium brutalist visual system; flag as niche-
  dependent.)
- Neural timing per slide: 0-0.3s visual contrast, 0.3-2s copy, 2-5s complex data, 5-8s
  simplify/CTA. Reads as a rationale for ONE focal point per slide with instant contrast, which
  matches the poster direction. TIER 2, single source.

## SO WHAT FOR US (how this changes what we build)

1. ENCODE THE CONCRETENESS SWEET SPOT AS A COVER-SLIDE RULE. The Nature meta-analysis (TIER 1)
   is the strongest thing in this whole division. The cover slide must be a PARTIAL reveal:
   specific enough that the payoff feels real, with exactly one thing withheld. The dominant
   real-world failure is OVER-concreteness (the cover answers itself, killing the swipe), and
   over-concreteness costs ~2x more than over-vagueness. Build a checklist item into the
   make-carousel skill: "Does the cover slide state the topic AND withhold the specific payoff?
   If a viewer could get the value without swiping, it is too concrete." This is a defensible,
   citable rule, not vibes.

2. TREAT SLIDE 2 AS A SECOND HOOK, NOT A CONTINUATION. The steepest drop is slide 1 to 3. Slide
   2 should re-hook (reduce skepticism, preview the structure, restate the payoff is coming),
   not just start listing. Design the deck so slides 1-4 are all doing curiosity work; the deck
   can relax after slide 4 where the curve flattens.

3. LAYER ONE MACRO-LOOP + MICRO-LOOPS. Open a named-destination macro-loop on the cover ("the
   one that pays $X is slide 7") and end most middle slides with a one-line micro-tease into the
   next. Reframe the driver as RESUMPTION PULL, not memory (per the 2025 Zeigarnik meta-
   analysis). We are manufacturing "I need to finish this," not "I'll remember this."

4. USE ODD COUNTS AND SHOW PROGRESS. Prefer 5/7/9-item decks. Put a visible "3 / 7" style
   progress marker: it is a quantified, closable curiosity gap and a chunking aid, both
   retention levers with real backing (chunking is TIER 1). Fits the brutalist/editorial system
   as a thin mono progress element, not a billboard.

5. MAKE THE LAST SLIDE EARN THE FOLLOW. Follows come from "this source closes gaps I care
   about." The final slide should (a) visibly close the macro-loop with a proportionate payoff,
   and (b) open one small forward loop ("next: X") to justify the follow. Do not end flat. Do not
   over-tease and under-deliver, it burns future-deck retention.

6. BUILD OUR OWN A/B RIG THE MOMENT PER-SLIDE INSIGHTS LAND. Nobody can publish competitor
   per-slide curves because that data is private (FINDING 8). Instagram is rolling out native
   per-slide swipe/completion insights in 2026. When Carter's account gets it, we log per-slide
   completion for every deck into a small tracker and A/B cover-slide concreteness (partial-
   reveal vs over-concrete vs over-vague) against the Nature prediction. This is the one place we
   can generate PROPRIETARY data the whole niche lacks. Highest-leverage build item.

7. POSTER DIRECTION IS PSYCHOLOGICALLY BACKED, NOT JUST AESTHETIC. Negative-space (+28% CTR),
   one-focal-point neural timing, and the pattern-disruption lift all point the same way as
   Carter's stated goal: one focal point per slide, near-zero body copy. The image-artifact
   decks that already won (47k-65k views, thousands of saves) are the visual pattern-interrupt
   the theory predicts. Keep pushing artifact/imagery cover slides over text templates; the
   90-day data and the psychology agree.

8. TREAT SAGUM'S SPECIFIC MULTIPLIERS AS EXPERIMENTS, NOT FACTS. The 3.7x, 34%, 28%, 2.3x
   figures are single-source (Sagum, one blog). Where they match Carter's brand (negative space,
   pattern disruption, one focal point) adopt them. Where they do not (the "native un-designed
   screenshot look"), ignore: it conflicts with the premium brutalist system and is niche-
   dependent.
