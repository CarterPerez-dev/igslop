## FINDINGS

### A. The image is recognized in 13 milliseconds, before the eye even moves

Mary Potter's lab at MIT (Potter, Wyble, Hagmann, McCourt, "Detecting meaning in RSVP at 13 ms per picture", Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics, 2014) showed subjects streams of 6 or 12 images at 13 to 80 ms each and asked whether a named concept ("picnic", "smiling couple") was present. Subjects performed above chance all the way down to 13 ms per image, the fastest they tested. Prior belief was that the brain needed at least 100 ms. Potter's interpretation: "what vision does is find concepts", and detection at 13 ms implies feedforward processing with no time for feedback loops.
Source: news.mit.edu/2014/in-the-blink-of-an-eye-0116 ; link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-013-0605-z

Implication chain: on a feed scrolling at 5 to 7 posts per second (see F below), the viewer extracts the GIST of slide 1 in a single feedforward pass, faster than a deliberate read. Whatever concept the slide broadcasts in that first pass is the only thing that decides stop-vs-scroll. A slide that requires reading to be understood has already lost, because reading has not started yet.

### B. Where the eye lands FIRST on a suddenly-appearing image: the center, not the top-left

Benjamin Tatler, "The central fixation bias in scene viewing" (Journal of Vision, 2007, PubMed 18217799), established that when a scene appears on screen, observers orient to the CENTER first, and this central fixation bias is strongest on the earliest fixations, independent of where the interesting image features actually are and independent of motor/saccade biases. Follow-up work ("Temporal evolution of the central fixation bias in scene viewing", JOV) found the bias is strongest at onset and decays to asymptote only after roughly 1000 to 2000 ms. The dominant trigger is the sudden image onset itself.
Source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18217799/ ; jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2661519

This is the single most important finding for slide 1. The classic F-pattern and Gutenberg "top-left entry" (Nielsen/Pernice 2010) describe how people read a TEXT-heavy page they have already decided to engage with. They do NOT describe the first ~1 second of a freshly-appeared image in a feed. In that first second, gaze is CENTER-biased. A feed slide is a sudden-onset image, so the center is the prime real estate for the first fixation, and only after 1 to 2 seconds does gaze migrate toward reading order.

### C. Faces, and especially threat-emotion faces, get automatic, pre-attentive capture

- General social salience: in the PMC eye-tracking study of 35 young adults (Tobii Pro Spectrum, 1200 Hz, simulated Instagram interface, 2025), human-centric and promotional elements drew the most gaze. Among interface chrome, the followers metric had the highest fixation duration (M = 0.663 s) over the logo (0.459 s) and buttons (0.135 s). Among content, "marketing content" (slogans, discount, product claims) topped fixation at M = 4.00 s, brand elements 3.94 s, influencer presence 3.29 s. Source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12641903/
- Threat faces are detected fastest of all: angry and fearful expressions are detected by the brain at roughly 90 ms versus about 500 ms for neutral/other expressions, and the amygdala acts as a fast "relevance detector" that biases sensory processing before conscious awareness and can override top-down attention. Source (macaque amygdala + human threat literature): ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8979552/ ; jneurosci.org/content/43/8/1405 (Rapid Processing of Invisible Fearful Faces in the Human Amygdala)
- Gaze cueing: a face looking AT something drags the viewer's attention to that thing (joint attention). Face presence plus gaze directed toward the product (versus gaze at the viewer) strongly raises attention, memory, brand evaluation, and purchase intention. Source: Frontiers in Psychology 2017 "Judgments at Gaze Value"; researchgate.net/publication/323283511 (Face Presence and Gaze Direction in Print Advertisements).

Implication: a human face on slide 1 is a near-guaranteed first-fixation magnet. A face with tension/intensity (not a bland smile) recruits the fastest attentional system. And the model's eyeline can be aimed at the headline word to hand attention off to the message.

### D. Contrast, color, and motion are the bottom-up salience levers

- Color vs black-and-white: in the MDPI Applied Sciences eye-tracking poster study (60 participants, 2026), color posters attracted and sustained more attention than black-and-white versions (F = 5.067, p = 0.028). Source (via search extract, page 403'd): mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/1/291
- Dynamic vs static: same MDPI study, dynamic posters massively outperformed static for total fixation duration (F = 245.896, p < 0.001) and fixation count. Motion is a potent bottom-up cue precisely because it contrasts with static neighbors in the same visual field. Source: same paper.
- Luminance contrast as stop-rate predictor (PRACTITIONER, weaker): marketing analyses (attributed to Buffer's 2026 algorithm guide, EarnifyHub, Mirra, surfaced via aestheticsofphotography.com "The Viral Visual Grammar of US Instagram in 2026") claim first-frame luminance contrast is the strongest single predictor of stop-rate, with peak-frame-1 luminance variance retaining ~40% more viewers. Treat the exact 40% as unverified industry lore; the DIRECTION (high luminance contrast in the opening frame stops scroll) is consistent with the academic salience literature. Source: aestheticsofphotography.com (403'd, via search extract).
- Contrast/accessibility floor (PRACTITIONER): design writeups cite a 7:1 text-to-background contrast ratio as a performance baseline and note contrast against darker backgrounds reads as "thumb-stopping". Source: designshifu / vibemyad 2026 color articles.

### E. Simplicity wins on two independent axes: fluency (preference) and clutter (efficiency)

- Processing fluency -> liking: Reber, Schwarz, Winkielman, "Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure" (Personality and Social Psychology Review, 2004). The more fluently a stimulus is processed, the more positive the aesthetic response. Figure-ground contrast, symmetry, figural goodness, and prototypicality all raise fluency and therefore liking. Beauty lives in the perceiver's ease of processing, not just the pixels. Source: pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf
- Clutter -> slower, worse search: the clutter literature (Moacdieh & Sarter; Rosenholtz-lineage work) shows visual clutter degrades search, raising fixation count, mean fixation duration, scan-path length, and first-saccade latency, and increases response time and error rate as data density rises. Source: sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003687019300924 ; sagepub 10.1177/1071181312561391

Together these are the hard science behind "poster, not newspaper." Fewer reading zones = higher fluency = more liking AND less clutter = faster gist extraction. Carter's own 90-day data agrees: image-artifact decks (one dominant visual) hit 47k to 65k views and 2,700 to 6,600 saves; busy text-template decks hit 10k to 18k views and 100 to 570 saves.

### F. How much time you actually get, and how fast the feed moves

- Feed scroll velocity: practitioner sources cite an average of 5 to 7 posts per second on Meta feeds, and Meta's 2025 Creative Best Practices report is cited for "first 1 to 3 seconds decide watch-vs-scroll" and hooks-in-first-3-seconds giving up to 89% higher completion than front-loading branding. Source: metamktgagency.com ; quickframe.com Facebook Reels 2026 guide. (PRACTITIONER, but directionally consistent with Potter's 13 ms gist.)
- Attention-span macro numbers (use skeptically, these are widely-circulated stats blogs, not primary studies): average single-post focus reported dropping from 12.1 s (2015) to 8.25 s (2025); a claimed 2026 MIT Media Lab/Stanford longitudinal figure of 7.6 s; LinkedIn attention "peaks at 4 s, drops 80% by 10 s"; average tweet dwell 1.7 s; Pinterest ~14 s per pin. Source: sci-tech-today.com, sqmagazine.co.uk, gitnux.org attention-span roundups. Flagged as low-trust in GAPS.

### G. Why a stopped scroll becomes a SAVE (the curiosity + utility engine)

- Information-gap theory of curiosity: Loewenstein (1994), summarized in Golman & Loewenstein (CMU). Curiosity fires when a person becomes consciously aware of a gap between what they know and what they want to know, producing an aversive "deprivation" state that motivates resolution. Partial knowledge is MORE motivating than total ignorance: once you know a question exists and know a little, the gap becomes concrete and urgent. fMRI shows the left caudate (dopamine reward pathway) responds when an information gap opens. Source: cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf ; journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.501
- Why people hit SAVE specifically: two documented motives. Type 2 "finish-later" saves are educational/information-heavy, almost infographic content the user cannot finish in the moment and wants to return to; Type 1 "super-like" saves are aspirational/reference (mood-board) saves of content "too good for just a like." A save is a stronger intent signal than a like because likes are reflexive double-taps while a save is a deliberate "I will come back to this." Source: medium.com/design-bootcamp Instagram saved-posts case study; later.com/blog/instagram-saves; stylist.co.uk.
- Carousel/save as ranking currency (PRACTITIONER, unsourced numbers, log as lore): carousels claimed at ~10% engagement vs 7% single-image vs 6% Reels; carousels "highest saves"; saves said to be top-weighted. No primary citation. Source: marketingagent.blog 2026 carousel article (self-admittedly uncited).

## SO WHAT FOR US (translate each into a slide-1 design rule)

1. Design for a 13 ms gist read, not a reading experience (from A). Slide 1 must broadcast ONE concept that is legible pre-attentively: one giant number, one badge/book/artifact image, one 3-to-5-word claim. If a human has to READ slide 1 to get it, the gist pass already failed. This is the scientific spine under "poster not newspaper."

2. Put the ONE focal point in the optical center, not the top-left (from B, Tatler central fixation bias). The feed slide is a sudden-onset image, so the first ~1 second of gaze is center-biased. Anchor the single hero element (the number, the face, the artifact) at or just above true center. Reserve the F-pattern/top-left logic for later slides that people actually read after committing. Do NOT bury the hook in a top banner.

3. Lead with the artifact image, and when a human is present, use an intense/tense face aimed at the message (from C). Badge/book/tool/screenshot imagery already outperformed for Carter. When adding a person, pick a high-tension expression over a bland smile (90 ms threat-detection channel) and point the eyeline at the headline word to hand off attention via gaze cueing. Never a stock grin looking at the camera.

4. Win on bottom-up salience the feed cannot ignore: high luminance contrast + saturated color + one motion moment (from D). Slide 1 should have the highest light/dark contrast of the deck and at least one saturated color block that pops against the muted grey/white feed chrome. Strongest lever available in-pipeline: because shotpls renders standalone HTML, we can export slide 1 (or an inserted slide) as a SHORT MP4/GIF loop, a single deliberate motion (a number counting, a bar filling, a term glitching in). The MDPI dynamic-vs-static effect (F = 245.896) is the biggest single effect size in this whole research set. An animated first slide inside the carousel is the highest-ROI build we are not yet doing.

5. Maximize processing fluency and kill clutter: one message per slide, 1 to 2 structural elements max, generous negative space (from E). This is double-justified: fluency raises LIKING (Reber) and low clutter raises SPEED and accuracy (clutter literature). Concretely: cap slide 1 at one hero + one label; push everything else to later slides. This matches Carter's own view-zoom density note (editorial pacing, thin rules, not billboard-stacked cards) and his 47k-vs-14k data.

6. Front-load everything into the first 1 to 3 seconds (from F). At 5 to 7 posts/sec, there is no warmup. No logo-first, no title-card runway. The concept-carrying element is the first thing rendered, full-bleed, top of visual weight. Branding is a small lockup, never the hook.

7. Engineer an information gap on slide 1 to earn the swipe, and load real utility to earn the SAVE (from G). Slide 1 headline should open a concrete, resolvable gap ("The 7 tools that got me hired", "What $95k SOC analysts actually do all day"): partial knowledge, named specifics, an obvious missing piece the reader now NEEDS. Then the interior slides must deliver genuinely reference-grade, infographic-quality information so the deck lands in the "finish-later / too-good-to-just-like" save bucket. Curiosity gets the stop and swipe; density-of-utility gets the save. Both are required; a curiosity hook over thin content gets swiped and forgotten.

Net build directives for the carousel lab:
- One focal point, optically centered, 13-ms-legible. This is now a hard rule for slide 1, backed by Potter + Tatler.
- Prototype an ANIMATED slide-1 export path (HTML -> short MP4/GIF via headless Chrome capture) as a first-class pipeline feature. Largest documented effect size in the set.
- Highest-contrast, most-saturated slide of the deck is slide 1.
- Real artifact imagery or a tense gaze-cueing face beats any text template.
- Headline = a concrete information gap; interior = reference-grade density that justifies a save.
